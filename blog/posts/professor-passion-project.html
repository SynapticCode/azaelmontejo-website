The Day I Collaborated on My Professor's Passion Project

January 2025 | Research, Collaboration, Computational Cognitive Science

The moment it clicked was not dramatic. We were in the lab, staring at a rough prototype, and Professor Natalia Velez asked, "Can this design force people to choose between team alignment and local adaptation without making it feel like a math test?" That question landed hard because it was exactly the problem we had been circling all week.

Last summer, I spent nine weeks at Princeton through the NSF REU, working in computational cognitive science with the Velez CoLab. The project was her long-running passion project: an experiment around the coordination-adaptation tradeoff, where teams perform better when they either synchronize tightly or adapt quickly, but rarely maximize both at the same time. On paper, the logic was elegant. In practice, we had to make people actually feel that tradeoff while making fast decisions.

Where Collaboration Became Real

Early versions were technically correct but hard to read as an experience. Participants could solve parts of the task mechanically, which weakened what we were trying to measure. I remember pitching a reframing: keep the scientific structure intact, but redesign the interaction so the tension between coordination and adaptation shows up naturally in the moment-to-moment choices.

That was the best part of working with Professor Velez. She pushed every assumption, but she did it in a way that made the idea better instead of shutting it down. We argued, tested, revised, and repeated until the task felt both rigorous and human.

What Changed for Me

I used to think good research started with brilliant answers. That summer taught me it starts with better questions, especially the uncomfortable ones that expose weak design choices. The real progress came from turning "this seems smart" into "this actually works when real people interact with it."

I also learned that my contribution was not separate from the technical work. Translating abstract models into intuitive systems is technical work. Building something people can use without diluting the science is technical work.

What I Carried Forward

That collaboration changed how I build now. Whether I am working on research tools, product ideas, or leadership systems, I look for the same signal: does the design create meaningful decisions under real constraints, or does it only look impressive in theory? That mindset came directly from those nine weeks.

Working on that passion project gave me more than a strong summer. It gave me a standard for how ideas should be developed: in conversation, under pressure, with people willing to challenge each other and still build in the same direction.
